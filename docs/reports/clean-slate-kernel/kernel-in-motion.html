02

Three persistence modes, side by side

A persistence mode chooses where a turn ends, and a turn commits where it ends; Ephemeral alone writes nothing when the run ends in its first turn and sent nothing. So the mode decides what a run costs and what a crash can lose. Three instances of one request-response workflow run at the same time, one per mode; one set of controls steps all three together, activity by activity.

    04

    The fence lab: try to make two owners both write

    Act two showed one paused owner bounced by the fence. Here you choose the order of events. Node A owns partition 17 at epoch 8 and has a batch to commit; Node B would take the partition if it could. Whatever you choose, at most one of them writes, and a commit lands at most once. The lease is the decided 10 s; times and positions are illustrative.

    Why the check is its own round trip. Sent in one packet, a fence that matched nothing does not stop what follows it: a COMMIT in the same packet would still land. So the owner must see "1 row" before it sends COMMIT, which makes 2 round trips on PostgreSQL. The fence travels one of two ways: in the batch, as EF concurrency tokens on the epoch and the position (the default: 2 round trips, the lease time from the owner's clock), or as its own statement first (3 round trips, the lease time from the database's), which is SQL Server's default since finding E9. Leases decide who should write. The fence decides who can. The invariant: after a take at epoch e + 1, nothing prepared under e commits, and the new owner sees everything that did commit.

    Built and measured in the prototype: both ways of sending the fence and their round trips; the take; the reset after an unknown outcome or a fence that matched nothing; a simulation that injects paused owners and lost answers and checks the invariant after every action; and a mutation check showing that the position decides only when a reset is missing (FINDINGS.md, section 3). Decided: the 10 s lease, the fence in the batch by default, a response released only after its commit; the fence as its own statement on SQL Server is decided but not applied in the prototype yet. Illustrative: the times and positions, and the moment Node B tries. The lab draws only the partition row's lock, with the fence apart from the payload as when it is its own statement; in the batch EF orders the statements, and on SQL Server the fence comes last, which is how E9 deadlocks.
